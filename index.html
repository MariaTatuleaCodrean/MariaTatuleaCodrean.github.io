MARIA TATULEA-CODREAN

ELS KOFFEMAN POSTDOCTORAL FELLOW

I construct mechanistic, computational, and material models to predict emergent behaviours in biological systems. My current research spans four themes: flagellar locomotion (multi-tail robots, microalgae rotors), motion in complex fluids (nematic liquid crystals, yield-stress fluids), synchronisation (of hydrodynamic and other origins), and self-organisation (bacterial aggregation, bacterial motor assembly). Previously, I focused on modelling the coupled hydrodynamics of bacterial flagella, a topic that remains close to my heart. My work is highly interdisciplinary, combining methods from fluid mechanics, biological physics, asymptotic methods, and dynamical systems. I especially enjoy collaborating with experimentalists and designing macroscopic-scale experiments that bring the physics of microorganisms to life.

I joined the Soft Matter Group at the University of Amsterdam as the inaugural Els Koffeman Postdoctoral Fellow in July 2025, after holding the Lynden-Bell Research Fellowship in Mathematics at Clare College, Cambridge for three years. Before that, I was a postdoctoral research associate of Prof. Jean-François Joanny at the Collège de France and carried out my doctoral studies in Prof. Eric Lauga's group at DAMTP, University of Cambridge.

Recent Research

Bacterial Aggregation

In our latest preprint, we explore how swimming bacteria aggregate in nematic liquid crystals, a complex fluid environment relevant to many biological and industrial settings. By combining experiments and theory, we show that aggregation is fundamentally limited by the bacteria's own swimming behaviour, offering new insight into how active matter organises itself in structured fluids.

Cell Adhesion

This collaborative study reveals how cells adhere to and spread on fluid membranes, driven by microtubule-dependent mechanotransduction. Using functionalized lipid bilayers as a model system, we uncover a physical pathway linking cytoskeletal dynamics to adhesion, and propose a mean-field theoretical model that explains the observed receptor clustering as a phase-separation process.

Multiflagellated Swimming

Does the swimming speed of a multiflagellated bacterium increase indefinitely with the number of flagella? In this paper, we show that hydrodynamic interactions between flagella and the limited torque-generating capacity of the bacterial flagellar motor cap the swimming speed once the flagellar number exceeds a critical threshold, reshaping how we think about multiflagellated swimming.
